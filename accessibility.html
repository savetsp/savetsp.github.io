Are the bars accessible?

Yes. The bars are open to everyone, year-round, for free. People with disabilities, people recovering from injury, and people on crutches train here.1 The courts and fitness area at Tompkins Square can be accessed by wheelchair from Avenue B. All the connected paths are firm and wide, and the bars sit on a surface Parks replaced two years ago.2 Maybe NYC Parks wants to improve the staircase on E 10th St, but it does not have to demolish the bars to do that.

What does the law say?

The federal ADA standards require an accessible route to exercise equipment and clear floor space at one of each type.3 They “do not require changes to exercise machines or equipment” (U.S. Department of Justice).4

What do people with disabilities ask for?

The academic research on accessibility shows that a main concern for people with disabilities, when visiting outdoor recreation areas, is how to get there and how to get around: paths, surfaces, transit, toilets, seating and shade.5–12 One study followed 35 midlife and older adults with mobility disabilities, nearly half of whom used wheelchairs. They named curb cuts, sidewalks, resting places, shade and restrooms as their top concerns.6 An audit of 10 parks and playgrounds in Delaware found gaps in transit and curb cuts.13 You don't need to demolish the bars to improve the access.

What about the equipment?

Complaints about equipment are best documented in indoor gyms and weight rooms,14,15 where people can't reach weights or cables.16 Outdoor studies that examine equipment are about machines and gear, not fixed bars.7,8,10 Fixed bars have no seats, hinges or movable parts. We found no study that asked wheelchair users about calisthenics bars.17,18 So we should ask a reasonable, unbiased sample of them, before anyone tears down the bars on their behalf.

What do Parks’ own records say?

NYC Parks keeps a detailed paper trail on every park: its inspectors’ findings, from February 1999 to August 2026, and its repair orders, from June 2009 to October 2026, all published on NYC Open Data. For 65 parks with adult fitness areas, Tompkins and 64 others, we pulled every record that uses the words ADA, accessible, handicap or wheelchair, and read what each one is about. The full method is in our analysis of Parks’ own records, where we also used these records to assess the bars’ durability.

Parks takes accessibility seriously, and its records show it. Across these 65 parks, they contain 2,145 records that use the words ADA, accessible, handicap or wheelchair to refer to people with disabilities.

What “raised surface” and “other” mean

Raised surface in an accessible area. Inspectors have a standard entry, “Safety Surface uplifted 1.5” in accessible area,” for rubber surface that has lifted enough to trip someone. Most are near swings and play equipment. A few are repair orders to fix it.

Other. Records that fit none of the rows above, such as “Pls relocate fire extinguisher for ADA compliance,” “Remove 1 bench at end of C+C tables to make it ADA,” and “Fall hazard due to accessible ladder leading to roof of Fireboat House.” Where a record clearly belonged in another row, such as a broken ADA faucet or a worn ADA swing strap, we moved it there.

Parks notices small things. At Tompkins, staff logged a rusted screw on the ADA swing and a broken stall door in the ADA restroom. Yet in all 2,145 records, none says a fitness area is inaccessible, at Tompkins or anywhere else. In fact, when inspectors wrote up the rubber surface under the Tompkins bars, in 2017 and 2019, they described it as an “accessible area.”

Parks installs signs so users know which areas are accessible. At Tompkins, inspectors counted accessible signs on 20 of 24 park-wide inspections from March 2008 to January 2019, one of them “adj MPPA,” the area that is now the skate park. The sign has since been removed.

So where do we stand?

We all support accessibility and inclusion. What we oppose is accessibility theater, replacing an area that already works for people of all abilities with one that might look better on paper but serves them worse.
