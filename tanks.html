Detention tanks: what the records say

NYC Parks officials say stormwater tanks tend to go under basketball courts. The city’s own records say otherwise: of 335 tanks citywide, 31 sit under a basketball court.

NYC Parks wants to rebuild the Tompkins basketball courts with a stormwater detention tank underneath, and its officials have argued that this is where such tanks usually go. Is it true? The city keeps a record of every tank, so we checked, and this note explains what we found.

Key findings. The NYC Department of Environmental Protection (DEP) records 335 underground stormwater tanks across the city, 238 built and 97 planned. Only 31 of them, about 9%, sit under a basketball court. Even on Parks property, where 148 of the tanks are, basketball courts hold 31, about one in five. Even counting every tank within 10 meters of a basketball court, it is 46 of 335. Parks’ claim is not supported by the official records.

1. What Parks said

At the Community Board 3 full board meeting on October 27, 2026, a board member asked whether the tank could be installed without rebuilding the whole basketball court and closing it. A NYC Parks official who spoke at the meeting answered:

“Another reason why the tanks tend to end up under basketball courts is because there are no tree roots under there. Because we never planted trees near the basketball court, just by nature of how basketball courts work.”

Quoted from our transcript of the meeting recording, lightly cleaned of repeated words.

2. The data

DEP keeps a public map of every stormwater tank it has built or plans to build. Each record gives the tank’s type, its status, its program and its location. We took every tank in DEP’s green infrastructure records and checked, for each one, two things.

  • Whose land it is on. Inside a Parks property, according to Parks’ property map. Otherwise, the owner of the tax lot, according to the city’s land records (PLUTO).
  • What sits on top. A court or field, according to Parks’ athletic facilities map. A children’s play area, according to Parks’ playground map. Or a parking lot, building or plaza, according to the city’s official maps of parking lots, buildings and plazas.

Each tank is counted once. All records are public on NYC Open Data, and our full table links every tank to its sources.

3. Where the tanks are

Parks hosts more tanks than any other landowner, but fewer than half of them. Of the 335, 148 are in parks. The rest are at NYCHA housing (86), schools (46) and other sites (55). Off Parks property, the most common spot is a parking lot.

4. Conclusion

NYC Parks officials told Community Board 3 that stormwater tanks tend to end up under basketball courts. DEP’s records show that fewer than one tank in ten does. In parks, tanks also go under softball fields, multipurpose areas, handball courts and children’s play areas, and many sit under none of these. The claim that tanks belong under basketball courts is simply not substantiated by the data.

5. Method and limits

  • Tanks. Every DEP green infrastructure record of type Subsurface Detention System, Subsurface Storage, Detention System, or Detention System (Connected to Sewer), downloaded in November 2026. Built means DEP status “Constructed.” Planned means “In Construction” or “Final Design.”
  • One point per tank. DEP records each tank as a single point. A tank may extend beyond the court or field over that point, and a tank recorded just outside a court may reach under it.
  • What sits on top. We checked the map layers in this order and took the first that contains the tank’s point: courts and fields, children’s play areas, parking lots, buildings, plazas. “None of these mapped features” may be a lawn, a path, a garden or a schoolyard, which these maps do not show. We do not guess which.
  • Overlapping outlines. Some basketball courts are drawn on top of another field, such as a softball field or a hockey rink. If any basketball court outline contains a tank’s point, we count the tank as under a basketball court.
  • Distance to the nearest basketball court. To allow for tanks that reach beyond their point, we also measured how far each tank’s point is from the nearest basketball court outline. Within 2 meters: 39 tanks. Within 5 meters: 41. Within 10 meters: 46. Within 20 meters: 67, or one in five. The full table gives each tank’s distance.
  • Current maps. The maps show what is there today, not what was there before a tank was built.
  • Coverage. DEP’s records cover its green infrastructure program. Tanks built outside it are not included.
