What Parks isn’t telling you

Parks has made many assertions, but did not present evidence. Here is what it is proposing, what we are asking, and why.

What is NYC Parks proposing?

On Oct 15, NYC Parks presented three options to CB3 for rebuilding and expanding the kids' pool at East 10th Street and Avenue B, and argued for Option 1. That option would demolish and rebuild the courts, the bars and nearby areas, closing everything for 18 months or more.

On Oct 27, the full Community Board 3 sent the plan back to its Parks Committee, which takes it up again on Nov 12.

What are we asking CB3 to do?

Vote for Option 3, which Parks already offered:

  • Rebuild the pool.
  • Build the stormwater tanks in a way that minimizes disruption to the basketball courts, and allows future maintenance without closing them again.
  • Preserve the existing workout equipment. Any proposed enhancements should be non-intrusive and supported by credible research.

We also ask that board members visit the bars, feel the equipment, and watch how people of all ages, races, genders and athletic abilities use them. You will understand why we love them and the community they nourish.

Did the community ask for this?

No. More than 3,500 people have signed a petition asking Parks not to demolish and rebuild the courts, the bars and adjacent areas, because they are not broken.

Are you against a bigger pool for kids?

No. Build a better pool.

Aren't the bars old and in bad shape?

They are 40 years old and remain as solid as new. Parks has never demonstrated they are unsteady, corroded or unsound. Its list of alleged defects centers on peeling paint, gaps in the safety surface, a couple of missing bolts, and “debris” that seems to be dry leaves. What it calls “rust” may be remnants of orange paint. Why condemn the equipment without a valid structural assessment?

Could the bars be repaired?

Yes. And that's what Parks used to do. It painted the bars in 2021, replaced the safety surface in 2024, and welded the bars when they lost a bolt. Repairing is cheaper, faster, and greener than demolition.

Parks says the bars are at the end of their useful life. Are they?

Parks has offered no evidence to support this claim, and admits that the equipment’s “structural integrity has not been determined” (slide 24).

Don't the bars have to be made accessible?

The experts we consulted are adamant that the bars are already accessible. People of every age, size and ability use them, including for rehab after injury. The equipment has wide clearance all around, including wheelchair-compatible clearance. Parks claims that the surface route is inaccessible, but Parks installed a fully accessible surface route two years ago. We support repairs and adjustments that improve access, without demolishing sound equipment.

At the same time, Parks should clarify which accessibility requirements it believes the existing installation fails to meet. What measurements or observations support that conclusion? Which of these problems could be corrected without replacing the existing equipment?

Shouldn't the equipment be ADA certified?

There is no such thing. The ADA establishes design standards for an area as a whole, and does not approve or certify equipment. An “ADA compliant” seal on a product is a manufacturer’s marketing claim.

Aren't the bars in violation of “city code”?

Parks said so, but its own report clarifies that these standards are optional and voluntary (slide 24), with the exception of “Lack of ADA accessible route,” which Parks remedied two years ago.

What about the drainage pipes?

Parks has mentioned this briefly, but did not provide enough details or share its findings. Can the pipe be serviced without demolishing the equipment on top?

Won't Parks build it back new and better?

New is not better. These bars are known around the world, and tourists come to the East Village to see them. Parks has promised it will try to reproduce the layout of the existing equipment to allow for the full range of current uses. We counted 168 separate bars, including 125 horizontal, 41 vertical and 2 arched, and each of them plays an important role. The law, however, constrains Parks along multiple dimensions on what it can buy or build. This means it cannot say with confidence what will happen.

Aren't Parks officials the experts?

Parks' track record on workout equipment is weak. The pull-up bars it installed in Chelsea were unstable from day one. The recently installed bar at Gulick Park has already been removed. New York once had three sets of tall, T-jointed parallel bars. Parks removed two and put in low, wide bars that restrict the range of motion, which defeats their purpose. At Hamilton Fish Park, the redesigned bars remain “sparsely used,” as the New York Times reported.

Why do the basketball courts have to close?

The courts were rebuilt in 2015 and repainted two years ago. Many players consider them the city's best. They are in the crosshairs because Parks wants to put stormwater tanks in the center of the area. Tanks might need maintenance, so the courts would close again every time they are serviced. What viable alternatives has Parks explored to minimize disruption to players?

Isn't a brand new park worth 18 months?

Eighteen months is a long time, and it might take longer. East River Park's outdoor gym is closed through 2027. The cheapest gym nearby costs $500 a year and does not allow kids. The YMCA is about $1,200 for one adult. Why not pause this project until East River reopens?

Who are we?

We are a diverse set of park users and neighbors of Tompkins Square Park. To support us, sign the petition, email public leaders, and follow @save.tompkins. Write to us at savetsp@gmail.com.
