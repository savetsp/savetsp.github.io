What Parks isn’t telling you

Parks has made many assertions, but did not present evidence. Here is what it is proposing, what we are asking, and why.

Download a printable version (PDF, one double-sided page) →

What is NYC Parks proposing?

On Oct 15, NYC Parks presented three options to CB3 for rebuilding and expanding the kids' pool at East 10th Street and Avenue B, and argued for Option 1. That option would demolish and rebuild the courts, the bars and nearby areas, closing everything for an extended period. On Oct 27, the full Community Board 3 sent the plan back to its Parks Committee. On Nov 12, the committee backed Option 2, the same rebuild done in two phases. The full board votes on Tue, Nov 24.

What are we asking CB3 to do?

Reject NYC Parks’ plan and ask for a better one, like the community’s plan:

  • Full ADA compliance now. Fix the rubber transition tiles Parks installed in 2024, which Parks admits are too steep, and mark the clear floor spaces the law requires.
  • Keep the bars in place, and be careful not to damage them. Community members will pay to weld any bar that comes loose.
  • If Parks adds adaptive equipment, integrate it with the bars on one continuous safety surface.
  • Before spending public money, ask people with disabilities what they would use.
  • Rebuild the pool, and build the stormwater tanks in a way that minimizes disruption to the basketball courts.

We also ask that board members visit the bars, feel the equipment, and watch how people of all ages, races, genders and athletic abilities use them. You will understand why we love them and the community they nourish.

Did the community ask for the bars and courts to be demolished and rebuilt?

No. More than 3,700 people have signed a petition asking Parks not to demolish and rebuild the courts, the bars and adjacent areas, because they are not broken.

Are you against a bigger pool for kids?

No. Build a better pool.

Aren't the bars old and in bad shape?

They are nearly 40 years old and remain solid. Parks admits their “structural integrity has not been determined” (slide 24). Its own records agree they are sound. Read our analysis here →

Could the bars be repaired?

Yes. And that's what Parks used to do. It painted the bars in 2019, replaced the safety surface in 2024, and welded the bars when they lost a bolt. Repairing is cheaper, faster, and greener than demolition.

Don't the bars have to be made accessible?

The bars are accessible. People of every age, size and ability use them, including for rehab after injury. The equipment has wide clearance all around, including wheelchair-compatible clearance, and Parks’ own inspectors have long called that spot “accessible” in internal records. One fix remains: the rubber transition tiles Parks installed in 2024, between the pavement and the safety surface, are too steep. Parks can fix them now, without demolishing anything. Read our note on accessibility → Which accessibility requirement does the installation fail, and can it be fixed without replacing the bars?

Shouldn't the equipment be ADA certified?

There is no such thing. The ADA establishes design standards for an area as a whole, and does not approve or certify equipment. An “ADA compliant” seal on a product is a manufacturer’s marketing claim.

Aren't the bars in violation of “city code”?

Parks said so, but its own report clarifies that these standards (ASTM F3101) are private, optional and voluntary (slide 24), with the exception of “Lack of ADA accessible route.” Parks replaced the surface in 2024; the transition tiles it installed are too steep, and Parks can fix them now.

What about the drainage pipes?

Parks mentioned this briefly but did not share its findings. Under Option 3, Parks itself proposed running a new drain line beside the bars, “minimally disrupting the existing fitness.”

Won't Parks build it back new and better?

New is not better. Parks’ own records on new equipment elsewhere are quite damning. These bars are known around the world, and tourists come to the East Village to see them. Parks has promised it will try to reproduce the layout of the existing equipment to allow for the full range of current uses. The law, however, constrains Parks along multiple dimensions on what it can buy or build. This means it cannot say with confidence what will happen.

Aren't Parks officials the experts?

Parks' track record on workout equipment is weak. At 40 of 64 other parks with fitness areas, its own records show serious, chronic or premature problems. The pull-up bars it installed in Chelsea were unstable from day one. At Hamilton Fish Park, the redesigned bars remain “sparsely used,” as the New York Times reported. We visited Macombs Dam Park, where the bars were rebuilt in 2020. We saw vestiges of bars that have already fallen apart, and saw how the remaining ones shake during regular use.

Why do the basketball courts have to close?

The courts were rebuilt in 2015 and repainted two years ago. Many players consider them the city's best. They are in the crosshairs because Parks wants to put stormwater tanks in the center of the area. Parks officials say tanks tend to go under basketball courts. Public records show only 34 of 335 do, about one in ten (read our note on tanks). Tanks might need maintenance, so the courts would close again every time they are serviced. What viable alternatives has Parks explored to minimize disruption to players?

Isn't a brand new park worth the wait?

Parks now plans to rebuild in two phases, after East River Park reopens. That still closes the bars for an extended period, and city projects often run late. Then the bars come back as replacements, with no guarantee they will hold. The cheapest gym nearby costs $500 a year and does not allow kids. The YMCA is about $1,200 for one adult. Where should the kids go? Where should adults go if they can’t afford a private gym or don’t want to pay for one? Why close down a hub that is so important for the community?
