What's happening

Parks got almost $10 million earmarked for the mini pool, and is now creating a problem that does not exist. It wants to also tear down the basketball courts, the calisthenics park and the shaded courtyard, and close them for almost two years.

Nobody asked for this. On the contrary, the community has been consistent, loud and clear. Repair and maintain. Do not demolish and replace what works.

On October 15, the CB3 Parks Committee fell for a presentation full of mischaracterizations and endorsed the worst of all options, which closes everything, for everyone, for almost two years. The full CB3 discusses it on October 27, and we have to be there to be heard. Council member Harvey Epstein can also fulfill his promise to park users and stop this nonsense.

What the community told Parks

This is not our spin. It is in Parks' own records.

  • January 29, community meeting. “Fitness bars are best in city. Do not change! These are loved!” “Fitness area is vital, don't close this area to the public at all.” “Do not close the basketball courts at all.” (Parks' notes)
  • May 16, tabling session in the park. “The general feedback was in favor of keeping the courts and fitness areas untouched.” “Phasing was requested to reduce the duration of closure.” “The tabling session confirmed the same feedback that was heard during the Community Input Meeting.” (Parks' minutes)

Parks heard us, loud and clear. Then it proposed to close everything, for everyone, for almost two years.

This plan does the reverse of what CB3 stands for

  • Less affordable. It takes away a free outdoor gym and courts, used every day by people who can't pay for a gym, for almost two years.
  • Less inclusive. It razes the courtyard where deliveristas rest in the shade, and officials call their presence "undesirable." That is hostile architecture.
  • Less accessible. It closes the area to everyone, including people with disabilities, for almost two years. Accessibility can be achieved with repair, without demolition.

Let's get a better plan

  • Respect the people who use the park. It does not count as listening if you don't listen
  • Upgrade the pool
  • Repair the courts and the calisthenics surface
  • Keep the courtyard open
  • Make the area more accessible
  • Never close everything at once
  • Preserve this area as an epicenter of wellbeing, affordability, friendship and community

What Parks isn't telling you

  • The "community priorities." Parks' October slides list "Upgrade Basketball Courts" under community priorities. Parks' own May minutes say the opposite: the community wanted the courts and fitness area untouched.
  • The basketball courts. There is no problem with them. Parks says it wants a place without tree roots for an underground stormwater tank. Why put it exactly in the middle of the two courts, to disturb both at the same time?
  • The calisthenics park. Look at what Parks calls defects. On slide 23, dry leaves on top of the bars are listed as "debris." Almost everything else is peeling paint or worn pavement, both put there by Parks itself, plus a missing bolt or two that have been fixed before and might take a welder 3 to 5 minutes. Why close it for almost two years?
  • The "code." The standards Parks says the current setup violates are voluntary and optional, as they admit in a footnote. The other problems are cosmetic, easy to fix, or fake.
  • Accessibility. Everyone wants the area to be more accessible, and park users have said so from the start. What Parks proposed is minor, just a couple sets of ADA equipment. No need to demolish everything for that.
  • The "flex space." Parks keeps calling a new "flex space" an improvement. The whole area is already flex. People train, play, stretch, jump rope, rest and meet friends there every day.
  • The courtyard. It is used mostly by deliveristas who need a shaded place to rest. If the problem is drug use, address it with adequate solutions, not hostile architecture. Keep the park inclusive.
  • The options. Parks presented three inadequate plans, mild variations of the same theme. Each one of them forced CB3 to choose between two evils. Why demolish when they could repair and maintain?
  • The consultants. The consultants Parks hired want to make the project as large as they possibly can. They misunderstood what they saw and created a presentation full of mischaracterizations, all in tiny font, so CB3 would agree with them.
  • The money. Most of the funding is a state grant for swimming pools. Is it even legal to spend pool money on demolishing and rebuilding basketball courts and a calisthenics park?
  • The risk. A major renovation could make the area slightly better, but it could also make it much, much worse. Rules about government contracting make it impossible to know beforehand how things will turn out. The closure will be disruptive no matter what, and the precedents from prior interventions are discouraging. The risk is entirely on the users, and we don't like these chances.

We are not arguing that nothing ever needs repair. We are arguing that Parks is misrepresenting the status of the park and using repairable problems to justify a much larger, more disruptive reconstruction. The result will be a city that is less affordable, less equal and less inclusive.
